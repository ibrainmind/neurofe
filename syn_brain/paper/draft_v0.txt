TITLE (working)
---------------

What can connectome-based neural mass models identify from stimulation-evoked EEG?
A synthetic teacher-student analysis of gradient-based Jansen-Rit fitting

Alternative:
Good fits, wrong circuits: identifiability limits of fitting Jansen-Rit models to TMS-EEG

ABSTRACT  (~250 words)
--------

Connectome-based neural mass models fitted to stimulation-evoked EEG are increasingly used to
draw mechanistic conclusions, for example that late TMS-evoked components arise from recurrent
network feedback, or that inhibitory parameters scale cortical excitability. These conclusions
rest on the fitted parameters meaning something. We tested this directly. A synthetic "teacher"
Jansen-Rit (JR) model with known parameters generated EEG responses to virtual stimulation, and
a "student" of identical form was fitted with the gradient-based (PyTorch, Adam) procedure used
in recent work. Because the truth is known, every fitted value could be checked. Combining
Fisher-information bounds, profile likelihoods and over 400 trained models, we find that only
two parameters, the stimulation gain and the excitatory synaptic rate, are reliably identified.
Three further parameters are fixed by exact scaling symmetries, and the excitatory and
inhibitory loop strengths are identifiable only as products, only with multi-condition
stimulation, and only when the column operates near firing threshold; at rest a JR column
behaves as a near-linear filter in which these loops are invisible. Fits that reproduce the
training data almost perfectly fail to predict held-out time windows (46-91% late-window error
with a perfect model and no noise), reproducing the generalization failures observed on real
TMS-EEG and intracranial data without any model misspecification. Identifiability depends
strongly on brain state: excitation-dominant columns saturate their excitatory loop, and the
quiet-to-active transition is bistable. Richer stimulation not only tightens the bounds but makes
the loss surface discriminative, so that the best-fitting model is also the most accurate. We
give a practical recipe and argue that mechanistic conclusions from such fits require
identifiability and robustness checks.

1  INTRODUCTION
---------------

1.1 Context.
  - Whole-brain neural mass models (NMMs) placed on a diffusion-MRI connectome are a standard
    tool for interpreting non-invasive brain stimulation responses (TMS-EEG) and intracranial
    stimulation (CCEPs) [Momi 2023; Momi 2025; David & Friston 2003 verify].
  - Gradient-based fitting through automatic differentiation (PyTorch) made subject-specific
    fitting fast and scalable [Griffiths 2022].
  - Published conclusions increasingly rest on fitted values and on in-silico manipulations of
    fitted models ("virtual lesions"): e.g. late TEP components (>100 ms) attributed to
    recurrent network feedback [Momi 2023]; an excitability gradient across the cortical
    hierarchy attributed to differential recurrence [Momi 2025].

1.2 The problem.
  - A model that reproduces data well may do so with many different parameter sets
    (non-identifiability, "sloppiness") [Gutenkunst 2007; Raue 2009 verify].
  - Validation in this literature is mostly reproduction of the training data (Pearson
    correlation, PCI); held-out tests are rare. Where they were run on real data (temporal
    hold-out, held-out stimulation site), fitted models failed:
      TMS-EEG temporal hold-out: Pearson 0.054 (vs 0.87 in-window)
      CCEP temporal hold-out:    Pearson 0.258 (vs 0.81 in-window)
      held-out stimulation site: 0.08-0.18 (vs 0.73-0.78 in-sample)
    [TODO: cite / include Ye's analysis; confirm numbers and datasets]
  - On real data three explanations cannot be told apart: non-identifiability, optimization
    failure, or model misspecification.

1.3 Approach and summary of findings.
  - Synthetic teacher-student design: the truth is known, so method failures can be separated
    from misspecification. We start with the smallest unit, a single JR column with a real
    leadfield read-out, where everything can be analysed exactly, then [TODO] the network.
  - Findings (one line each, matching sections 2.2-2.7).


2  RESULTS
----------

2.1 A teacher-student framework for stimulation-evoked responses  [FIG 1]
  - Teacher: JR column (neurofe's equations, nominal parameters), Euler 0.1 ms, read out through
    a real 62-channel leadfield column (PyTepFit anatomy); EEG in microvolts. Simulator matches
    neurofe's forward pass to 5e-15 uV.
  - Student: identical form, fitted by Adam through the simulator, from random starts (+-50%).
  - Virtual TMS: 12 conditions (amplitudes 250-4000, 2 / 10 / 30 ms pulses, paired pulses at
    30 and 100 ms, 10 Hz train, injection into excitatory and inhibitory populations, tonic
    background drive). Protocols combine conditions (single pulse; DOSE = 3 amplitudes;
    RICH = 6 conditions incl. E+I injection).
  - Noise: noise-free (best case) and 0.42 uV, the baseline noise of real trial-averaged TMS-EEG.

2.2 At rest, a JR column is a near-linear filter  [FIG 2]
  - With no background input the pyramidal membrane potential rests at E - I = -1.9 mV, far
    below threshold (v0 = 6 mV); sigmoid slope 0.03 of a possible 0.70.
  - Consequences: P1 amplitude 15.1 uV set by k*A only (elasticity +0.99 / +1.02); P1 latency
    (15 ms) set by the excitatory rate a only (elasticity -0.64); dose ratio 0.99 and
    paired-pulse ratio 1.00 (responses scale and superpose); the E-I loop leaves only a 0.61 uV
    late dip, comparable to the real noise level.
  - Linearisation explains why: V(s) = A k H_a(s) U(s) / (1 - A S' H_a(s)[L_E H_a(s) - L_I H_b(s)]),
    L_E = A c1 c2 S'_E, L_I = B c3 c4 S'_I. The data constrain only A*k, a, b and the loop gains
    L_E, L_I, each multiplied by sigmoid slopes that are tiny at rest.

2.3 Exact symmetries and sloppy directions  [FIG 3]
  - Two near-exact symmetries leave the EEG unchanged (< 1.5 uV of 60 uV for scale factors
    0.7-1.5; residual only from the tanh input bounds):
      (A, B) x lam  with (c1..c4, k) / lam        ("bigger synapses" = "more synapses")
      vmax x lam    with (A, B) / lam and k x lam
    A, B and vmax are therefore conventions, not measurable quantities.
  - Cramer-Rao bounds at 0.42 uV noise, single neurofe-style pulse:
      k 0.89%, a 2.5%, b 14%, c1 56%, c2 112%, c3 129%, c4 252%.
    Fisher eigenvalues span 5-6 orders of magnitude (sloppy).
  - Stimulation design helps, unevenly: RICH gives b 0.15%, c1 7.5%, c2 11%, c3 25%, c4 51%;
    E+I injection alone pins b to 0.38%; pulse width/shape changes little.
  - Products are much better constrained than ratios (c1*c2 vs c1/c2; c3*c4 vs c3/c4).

2.4 Backpropagation recovers stiff parameters and leaves the rest where it started  [FIG 4]
  - 402 single-node models (experiments E1-E8). Median "progress" toward the truth
    (1 = reached, 0 = unmoved): k 0.99, a 0.95, b 0.76, c3 0.61, c4 0.16, c1 -0.04, c2 -0.68
    (c2 moves away from the truth, absorbing residual error).
  - Final relative loss ~2e-5, far below the noise-equivalent threshold: the optimizer finds an
    EEG indistinguishable from the teacher's, with the wrong circuit.
  - Profile likelihoods: k, a, b have minima well above the noise threshold; c1-c4 stay below it
    between 0.5x and 2x the truth (single pulse); RICH lifts c1 and c3 only beyond about +-20%.
  - With realistic noise, c-errors fall BELOW their Cramer-Rao bounds: the parameters did not
    move, so their error simply reflects the starting point (or prior).
  - The parameterisation used by the published framework (Adam on raw values, lr 0.05) moves
    every parameter by ~0.05 per step, i.e. 0.05% of a or c1 but 0.5% of k. Same data and
    starts: ~250x higher final loss than log-scaled parameters; error on a 9.5% vs 0.6%, on k
    2.5% vs 0.07%. This alone explains "k recovered, everything else stuck" in network fits.
  - Releasing A, B, v0, r, vmax (12 free parameters): equally good fits, with A and k drifting
    along A*k = constant.

2.5 Good fits do not generalize, even with a perfect model  [FIG 5]
  - Whole-window prediction of held-out stimuli: 0.5-5% error (dominated by P1, set by k and a).
  - Late window (> 60 ms after the pulse): 5-30% error, even for stimuli seen in training;
    15-30% for the same stimuli delivered in a different brain state.
  - Temporal hold-out (fit 0-60 ms, predict the rest; noise-free, perfect model): late-window
    error 5.2% -> 91% (single pulse), 95% (DOSE), 28% (RICH). Median Pearson stays 0.81-0.94
    because the late shape survives while its amplitude is wrong; worst start r = 0.02. Pearson
    alone overstates the fit.
  - In the active state (p = 150): temporal hold-out 46%; held-out stimulus amplitude 24%.
  - Which parameter errors matter: per-model parameter error correlates with generalization
    error for the identifiable quantities (a rho = 0.78, c3*c4 0.71, b 0.65, c1*c2 0.51,
    k 0.47) but not for individual c1 (rho = -0.14), as the linear theory predicts.
  - Interpretation: the real-data generalization failures (section 1.2) are what this
    method produces without any misspecification.

2.6 Brain state determines identifiability: background drive, E/I balance, bifurcations [FIG 6]
  - Background drive p (mean afferent input; absent in diffusively coupled networks): above
    p ~ 115 the resting state jumps to an active, alpha-resonant branch (E - I ~ v0, slope 0.70);
    single-pulse bound on c3 falls from 129% to 4.7%.
  - E/I balance m (c1, c2 x sqrt(m); c3, c4 / sqrt(m); m^2 = excitatory/inhibitory loop ratio),
    15 x 19 grid of (p, m):
      * the quiet/active boundary bends with balance; sustained oscillation in a band of
        slightly inhibition-leaning columns (m ~ 0.55-0.95, p >~ 160, 6-11 Hz);
      * CRB medians by region, single pulse -> RICH:
          balanced    c1*c2 24% -> 9%,     c3*c4 22% -> 5%
          E-dominant  c1*c2 2807% -> 19%,  c3*c4 3329% -> 10%
          I-dominant  c1*c2 110% -> 15%,   c3*c4 36% -> 4%
      * mechanism for E-dominant failure: the excitatory interneurons saturate (sigmoid slope
        0.000 vs 0.106 balanced, 0.300 I-dominant), so c1*c2 cannot affect the output.
  - Bistability and hysteresis: at balance the quiet and active states coexist for
    p in [36.5, 114.6); a column switched on stays active down to p ~ 40-50 while a fresh start
    at the same drive is quiet. A student with slightly wrong parameters can sit on the other
    branch; near the fold, fits are multi-modal (p = 120: c3*c4 error 52-59% vs a 17% bound;
    warm-starting through the transition makes fits bimodal rather than better).
  - Integrator effects near the Hopf boundary: at m = 1, p = 270 the equations are (weakly)
    stable but neurofe's 0.1 ms Euler step sustains a 1.2 mV oscillation that decays with a
    10x finer step; genuine limit cycles (m = 0.8) are unchanged.
  - Robustness across subjects (4 teachers +-30%, p = 150): c3*c4 7-28%, c1*c2 13-95%.

2.7 Richer stimulation makes the loss surface discriminative  [FIG 7]
  - Hardest case (E-dominant), 20 restarts: with a single pulse, final loss does not predict
    c1*c2 accuracy (Spearman rho = +0.09, p = 0.70); the single best-fitting model had 64%
    error. With RICH, loss predicts accuracy (rho = +0.83 / +0.81, p < 0.001) and the
    lowest-loss start is the most accurate (3.1% / 0.8%).
  - Hard box bounds on c1-c4 roughly double the error (26.2% -> 63.6%), despite containing
    the truth.
  - Practical recipe: log-scaled parameters; multi-condition stimulation (amplitudes, paired
    pulses, E+I targeting); an active operating point away from both transitions; >= 20
    restarts; select by lowest loss; report identifiable combinations with uncertainty.

3  DISCUSSION
-------------

3.1 What TMS-EEG can and cannot tell a JR model.
  - Reliable: how strongly the cortex responds (k*A) and how fast excitatory synapses act (a);
    inhibitory kinetics (b) with the right protocol.
  - Conditional: loop strengths, only as products, only in an active state, only with
    multi-condition stimulation; not robust across subjects.
  - Never: synapse strength vs number (A, B vs c's), maximum firing rate.

3.2 Implications for published mechanistic conclusions.
  - Late TEP components as network recurrence (virtual lesions): in a quiet JR column late
    activity must come from the network, but an active column rings locally for ~0.5 s; the
    attribution therefore depends on the operating point and on the least identifiable
    (late-window) parameters. Virtual-lesion conclusions should be shown to hold across the
    set of equally good fits.
  - Fitted inhibitory time constants correlating with TEP features: b is the best-behaved of
    the inhibition-related parameters, but it directly sets late-dip amplitude, so part of the
    correlation is built into the fit (the original authors note the effect does not survive
    correction).
  - Fitted connectomes that stay at their tractography prior (as reported in Momi 2025) are
    the expected outcome when per-edge weights are not identifiable; conclusions then reflect
    the prior.
  - Tone: these papers' data-level findings and tools are unaffected; the point is which
    model-based inferences are supported. [TODO: agree framing with co-authors]

3.3 Recommendations.
  - Report validation beyond training-data fit: temporal hold-out, held-out stimulation site,
    held-out stimulus.
  - Optimise log-scaled parameters; fix gauge parameters; fit identifiable combinations;
    fractional delays; check results against integrator step near transitions.
  - Design stimulation for identification (multi-condition, E/I-targeted); choose operating
    points away from bifurcations; supply mean drive in network models.
  - Prefer ensembles with loss-based selection, or posterior methods, over single fits.

3.4 Relation to prior work.  [TODO: literature review]
  - Sloppiness in systems biology [Gutenkunst 2007]; profile likelihood [Raue 2009];
    identifiability and model comparison in DCM [verify]; simulation-based inference for
    whole-brain models [Hashemi 2023 verify]. Our novelty: the specific, widely used
    TMS-EEG pipeline; the mechanism from the equations; the dependence on brain state and
    stimulation design; reproduction of real-data generalization failures.

3.5 Limitations.
  - Mostly single node; rung R0 (teacher and student share the model form) and R1 (noise);
    misspecification rungs R2-R6 not yet run.
  - One optimizer family (Adam) for the main results.
  - Teacher realism: synthetic TEPs lack late activity until mean drive is added.
  - Neural mass models lump cell types; biological readings of parameters are approximate.


4  METHODS  (outline)
---------

4.1 Model. JR equations as implemented in neurofe (effective-value floors, tanh input bounds
    500, Euler 0.1 ms with 10 sub-steps per 1 ms sample, EEG = 5e-4 * leadfield * (E - I));
    tonic drive p and E/I multiplier m as defined above. Code: core/node.py, core/simulator.py,
    core/diffsim.py; equivalence tests in tests/test_simulator.py.
4.2 Anatomy and read-out. PyTepFit Schaefer-200 connectome, distances and 62-channel
    leadfield (external/pytepfit/).
4.3 Stimulation protocols. Table of the 12 conditions and protocols (scripts/single_node_tms.py).
4.4 Identifiability analysis. Central-difference Jacobians in log-parameter space; Fisher
    information F = J^T J / sigma^2 with sigma = 0.42 uV; Cramer-Rao bounds, eigen-spectra,
    combination bounds; profile likelihood by pinning one parameter and refitting the rest.
4.5 Training. Adam on log(theta/nominal), lr 0.04, cosine schedule, 300 iterations, relative
    MSE loss; random starts +-50%; raw-parameter ablation with neurofe's settings (lr 0.05).
    Experiments E1-E12 and the follow-ups as listed in reports/DECISIONS.md.
4.6 Bifurcation analysis. Fixed points by root-finding of a scalar equation; stability from
    numerical Jacobian eigenvalues; regimes confirmed by direct simulation (two time windows;
    10x finer step).
4.7 Metrics. Relative RMSE and Pearson on whole and late windows; progress toward truth;
    Spearman correlations between parameter and prediction errors.
4.8 Code and data availability. Repository, commit hash, one-command reproduction
    (scripts/run_single_node_tms.sh). [TODO: public release plan]

8  POSSIBLE COMPANION PAPER: THE ATTRIBUTION TEST
-----------------------------------------------

  Question: does fitting + virtual lesioning correctly attribute late TEP components to local
  vs network mechanisms?
  Design: synthetic teachers where the answer is known (late activity from an active local
  column vs from network feedback with quiet nodes); fit with the published pipeline; apply the
  same virtual lesions; score attribution across >= 20 equally good fits per teacher.
  Outcome either way is informative; natural to do jointly with the original method authors.
